## Objectives
The primary objective for this week was to study and perform R&D on the Clearpath Jackal robot platform and develop a similar compact differential skid-steer robotic chassis capable of handling an approximate payload of 25 kg.

The robot is being designed as a modular plug-and-play platform capable of supporting components such as LiDAR, robotic arms, cameras, and other testing equipment for different robotics applications.

This week mainly focused on:
* Ideation and drivetrain planning
* CAD design of the chassis
* Structural analysis and validation
* Initial manufacturing and fabrication of the chassis

The chassis was designed while considering compactness, structural strength, modularity, and ease of future modifications.

---

## Ideation
The project began with an introductory discussion with our mentor, Siddharth, where we were instructed to study the design and functionality of the Clearpath Jackal robot, as our goal was to develop a similar compact and robust robotic platform. One of the major constraints was maintaining dimensions similar to the Jackal UGV while designing a robot capable of handling an approximate payload of 20–25 kg.

Initially, Sushant and Aranis started the research and ideation process while Krish joined later due to college examinations. During this phase, Sushant studied the Jackal robot and prepared a checklist of important design considerations, drivetrain configurations, and structural requirements that the team should follow.

The mentor initially suggested constructing the chassis entirely using 20x20 aluminium extrusion profiles. Multiple chassis layouts and drivetrain configurations were discussed before finalizing a differential skid-steer drive system using chain transmission, where two motors control four wheels. Due to space constraints within the maximum chassis dimensions of 500 mm × 500 mm, the motors were placed diagonally while dead shaft assemblies were positioned on the opposite side for power transmission.

Several ideas were proposed during the discussions:
* A solid metal base plate for the chassis was suggested but rejected due to concerns regarding bending under impact loads and reduced structural efficiency.
* Removable motor mounting plates were proposed, allowing the entire motor assembly to be detached or repositioned easily for future modifications and maintenance. This modular approach was approved by the mentor.

Before beginning the CAD work, the team sketched multiple concepts on paper to communicate ideas effectively and evaluate different drivetrain and chassis arrangements.

## CAD Design
This stage marked the beginning of the actual development process, where the team started creating CAD models of the provided mechanical components and chassis assemblies. Sushant primarily handled the chassis CAD design, while Krish worked on the drivetrain assembly and Aranis contributed to both design and manufacturability improvements.

The first task involved modelling components such as:
* Dead shaft assemblies
* 775 series motors
* Chain and sprocket system
* Bearings and couplers

One major challenge during this stage was the lack of accurate CAD models available online for several components. In many cases, only technical drawings were available, requiring the team to manually model parts using dimensions from datasheets. Some components did not even have proper drawings available, so physical measurements had to be taken manually using rulers and calipers before recreating the parts in CAD software.

The initial chassis CAD was designed using 20x20 aluminium extrusion dimensions. However, during manufacturing preparation, the team discovered that the actual profiles had outer dimensions of 24x24 mm, while 20x20 mm represented only the inner dimensions. Due to this mismatch, the chassis had to be redesigned to accurately reflect the real extrusion geometry.

Several chassis concepts were explored during the design phase:
1. **"X" truss arrangement**
2. **Diamond-shaped reinforcement structure**

Both concepts incorporated diagonally aligned shafts because triangular and diagonal load paths provide significantly better rigidity and load distribution under impact conditions. Using finite element analysis (FEA), the team later determined that a hybrid combination of both designs provided the best structural performance.

### Finalized Specifications
**Chassis dimensions:** ~371 mm × 371 mm  
**Drivetrain configuration:**
* 4 × 775 Series IG Gearbox Motors
* 4 × 2.50-4 Wheels
* #25 Chain and Sprocket Transmission
* 2 × 14T Sprockets
* 2 × Dead Shaft Assemblies

After finalizing the basic chassis structure, another CAD assembly was created containing all major components to visualize the layout and identify mounting challenges. The team realized that mounting motors and dead-shaft assemblies directly onto the chassis would weaken the aluminium profiles due to excessive drilling.

To solve this, removable mounting plates were introduced, providing:
* Reduced damage to the structural chassis members
* Easier drilling and assembly
* Improved modularity and maintenance
* Flexibility in material selection for non-structural components

Initially planned to be manufactured using mild steel sheets, the unavailability of laser cutting facilities led to a design modification for additive manufacturing using Nylon Carbon Fiber filament. To make the motor mounts suitable for 3D printing, plate thickness was increased to 5 mm, reinforcing ribs/fillets were added, and a +0.2 mm tolerance was included for printer inaccuracies. 

### Software and Workflow
The team used both SolidWorks and Fusion 360 during the CAD development phase. Aranis utilized SolidWorks for detailed part modelling and constraint-heavy assemblies, while Sushant and Krish primarily used Fusion 360 for designing and iterative development. Fusion 360 allowed for easier collaboration, seamless file sharing, and faster conceptual iterations. 

**Problems Faced During CAD:**
Most available online CAD models for the 775 series motors had incorrect dimensions, especially around the gearbox and encoder sections. The team solved this by importing separate CAD models for the motor and gearbox, while manually modelling the encoder section as a simplified solid body for accurate positioning.

## FEA Analysis
The purpose of the Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was to evaluate whether the proposed chassis structures could withstand operational loads, vibrations, and impact forces while maintaining sufficient structural rigidity.

Before running the simulation, the CAD model was simplified:
* Small/non-critical features were removed.
* Hollow aluminium extrusion profiles were approximated as solid members for efficient meshing.
* Chassis links were merged into a single rigid assembly.

**Key properties defined (Aluminium):** Young’s Modulus, Poisson’s Ratio, Density, Yield Strength.

Fixed constraints were applied at the central mounting region, while operational loads were applied at the corners and motor mounting locations. Additional lateral impact loads were considered to simulate rough terrain usage. The simulations helped identify regions with stress concentration, allowing the team to optimize load distribution and increase truss reinforcement before fabrication.

## Manufacturing
The base material used for fabrication was square aluminium extrusion profiles (24 mm × 24 mm outer, 20 mm × 20 mm inner hollow). Due to a miscommunication, the team initially assumed the outer dimensions to be 20 mm x 20 mm, resulting in unnecessary challenges when trying to reduce the dimensions of already cut parts.

### Fabrication and Assembly
Profiles were cut using an angle grinder, requiring careful handling and safety gear (glasses and gloves) to mitigate risks of flying metal fragments and sharp edges. 

**Tolerances and Fitment:**
Achieving exact CAD dimensions is difficult with manual cutting. A 1 mm tolerance gap was initially maintained but proved insufficient, leading to slightly oversized parts. The team performed iterative trimming operations and extensive deburring to ensure clean joints and proper fitment.

**Assembly Layout:**
Profiles were arranged on a 1:1 scale printed draft over a wooden base for visual alignment. During assembly, manually cut L-brackets resulted in inconsistent lengths, causing alignment errors. Additionally, the wooden base mounting system was not included in the original CAD model, leading to unexpected constraints. To improve stabilization, Aranis designed a custom U-clamp that was 3D printed for dimension verification.

**Welding Fixture Challenges:**
The chassis needed to be flipped during welding, requiring a rigid but removable fixture. Acrylic plates and wooden sandwich fixtures were rejected due to poor load distribution. The team ultimately used additional aluminium extrusion profiles as temporary reinforcement, clamped with T-nuts and corner clamps, ensuring uniform force distribution and preventing frame warping.

## Problems Faced and Solutions

1. **Inaccurate Cutting and Tolerances**
   * *Problem:* Manual cutting led to dimensional inaccuracies.
   * *Solution:* Introduced controlled re-measurement, iterative trimming, increased tolerance margins, and used a 1:1 printed layout.
2. **Excess Burr Formation**
   * *Problem:* Sharp edges made assembly unsafe and difficult.
   * *Solution:* Mandatory deburring after every cut using flat files and grinders.
3. **Misalignment Due to L-Brackets**
   * *Problem:* Manually cut L-brackets caused chassis misalignment.
   * *Solution:* Measured brackets before installation, reworked inconsistent pieces, and planned future CAD-integrated bracket constraints.
4. **Missing Mounting Considerations**
   * *Problem:* Base and fixtures weren't in the CAD model.
   * *Solution:* Updated assembly approach, introduced custom U-clamps, and planned full fixture integration for future CAD.
5. **Welding Fixture Stability**
   * *Problem:* Required structural stability while flipping during welding.
   * *Solution:* Adopted an aluminium extrusion-based reinforcement frame for uniform force distribution.
